When you cannot forgive

Base Text

"You wicked servant! I forgave you all that debt because you pleaded with me. Should you not also have had mercy on your fellow servant?" Matthew 18:32–33

Forgiving can sound simple until the hurt has a name, a date, and consequences. Jesus never treated evil as something insignificant. Precisely for that reason, his teaching about forgiveness is so profound.

In Matthew 18, Peter asks how many times he must forgive. Jesus answers with a parable about two debts of vastly different proportions. One servant receives forgiveness for a debt impossible to repay, but afterward refuses to show mercy over a far smaller debt.

The point is not to say that what others did to us "was nothing." The debt exists. The point is that someone who has understood the magnitude of the mercy received from God can no longer relate to others as though he himself had never needed mercy.

Forgiving also does not mean calling evil good, automatically removing consequences, restoring trust without repentance, or remaining exposed to abuse. Scripture can speak at the same time about forgiveness, justice, correction, and boundaries.

Christian forgiveness gives up becoming the owner of vengeance. It hands judgment over to God and refuses to keep feeding the debt in the heart indefinitely.

That can be a painful process. But the starting point is not first to look at the offender. It is to look at how much we ourselves have been forgiven.

At the cross we see both things: God does not trivialize sin, and at the same time he opens a way of reconciliation for sinners.

We do not forgive because the injury was small. We forgive from the immensity of the grace we ourselves have received.

Prayer

Father, you know the harm that was done to me. Do not let pain become the ruler of my heart. Make me remember your mercy and teach me to forgive without calling evil good. Amen.
